\section{Numerical study}\label{sec:blup:numerical_study}

\color{magenta}
In this section, we present a finite-sample Monte Carlo simulation study and two real data applications. The simulation study is based on national electricity consumption curves for France, available from the ODR\'E platform \citep{odre_data}. These curves are used to estimate the parameters of a first-order autoregressive process (FAR(1)), which are then used to generate synthetic curves for the Monte Carlo simulations. For the real data applications, we analyse daily curves of intraday traded volume on a cryptocurrency perpetual futures contract, publicly available from the ByBit exchange, and daily national electricity consumption and production curves from the same ODR\'E platform. All results were obtained using the \texttt{R} package \texttt{adaptiveFTS} and supplementary code, both of which are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/hmaissoro}. An Epanechnikov kernel ($K(u) = (3/4)(1 - u^2)$ for $|u| \leq 1$, and $0$ otherwise) is used in all experiments.
\color{black}

\subsection{\color{magenta}Simulation design\color{black}}\label{blup:sec:simu_exp}
The Monte Carlo simulation is based on France's daily national electricity consumption curves from June to September 2024. The dataset contains 122 curves, one for each day within this period. Each curve represents electricity consumption recorded at 96 evenly spaced time points throughout the day, with measurements taken every 15 minutes. For readability, we normalise the curves to the interval $[0,1]$ by dividing each value by the maximum consumption observed over the entire period. Figure~\ref{fig:conso_last_curves} displays the last \color{magenta}three\color{black} consumption curves from the dataset.

\color{magenta}
To simulate FTS with patterns similar to those of the observed curves, we estimate the parameters of a FAR(1) model from the data and use them to generate synthetic FTS samples. We consider the FAR(1) process with an associated innovation process $\{\xi_n\}$, defined as:
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{blup:FTS_mod1}
	\color{magenta}X_n(u) = \mu(u) + \int_0^1\psi(u,s)(X_{n-1}(s)-\mu(s))\,ds + L_t\xi_n(u),\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
where the mean function $\mu$ and the kernel of the autoregressive operator $\psi$ are estimated from the real consumption curves. The innovation process $\{\xi_n\}$ is independently generated as a multi-fractional Brownian motion, with the Hurst index function also estimated from the real consumption curves \citep[see][]{taqqu2006}. Let us note that, as shown in Example 2 of \citet{Maissoro2025}, if the kernel $\psi(\cdot,s)$ is Hölder continuous with a Hölder exponent greater than the regularity of $\{\xi_n\}$, then the FTS $\{X_n\}$ inherits the regularity of the innovation process. In particular, $\{X_n\}$ has a local Hölder exponent corresponding to the Hurst index function of $\{\xi_n\}$ and a Hölder constant $L_t^2$.

\subsection{\color{magenta}(Auto)covariance function estimation\color{black}}\label{sec:emp_study:autocov}

\color{magenta}
We conclude this section with a performance comparison of the new autocovariance estimator against the \texttt{MPV25} estimator, which uses a single bandwidth. Let $\widehat{c}_{N,\ell}^{(i)}$ denote our new estimator computed from the $i$-th replication ($i = 1, \ldots, R = 400$), and let $\widehat{c}_{\texttt{MPV25}, \ell}^{(i)}$ denote the corresponding \texttt{MPV25} estimator. For benchmarking, we use the empirical infeasible estimator, denoted by ${\widehat{c}}_{N', \ell}$, which is computed on a large sample of size $N'$. This estimator is feasible in simulation settings. That is,
\color{black}
\begin{equation}
	\color{magenta}\widehat{c}_{N', \ell}(s, t) = \frac{1}{N' - \ell} \sum_{n=1}^{N' - \ell} \left\{X_{n}(s) - \widehat{\mu}_{N'}(s)\right\} \left\{X_{n+\ell}(t) - \widehat{\mu}_{N'}(t)\right\},
	\qquad \text{where} \quad
	\widehat{\mu}_{N'}(t) = \frac{1}{N'} \sum_{n=1}^{N'} X_n(t).\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
Then, for each $i = 1, \ldots, R$, we compute the pointwise error ratio:
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_ratio_autocov}
	\color{magenta}\forall (s, t) \in I^2, \quad
	E_\ell^{(i)}(s, t) = \frac{\left| \widehat{c}_{N,\ell}^{(i)}(s, t; h_\ell^*(s|t), h_\ell^*(t|s)) - \widehat{c}_{N',\ell}(s, t) \right|}{\left| \widehat{c}_{\texttt{MPV25}, \ell}^{(i)}(s, t; h^*) - \widehat{c}_{N',\ell}(s, t) \right|}.\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
The benchmark is computed once, outside the Monte Carlo. A single series of $N' = 5000$ curves is generated from \eqref{blup:FTS_mod1} with the same parameters, on the equidistant grid of $99$ points between $0.01$ and $0.99$ common to every curve, and is retained without measurement error. It stands in for the unknown $c_\ell$, so that the two estimators are compared through their distance to it rather than to each other.
\color{black}

\color{magenta}
We report the comparison at four pairs $(s,t)$, chosen so that the distance between the two local exponents takes a different value at each of them. At $(0.7, 0.8)$ we have $(H_s, H_t) = (0.68, 0.72)$, at $(0.2, 0.4)$ we have $(0.48, 0.56)$, at $(0.4, 0.7)$ we have $(0.56, 0.68)$, and at $(0.8, 0.2)$ we have $(0.72, 0.48)$. The four values of $|H_s - H_t|$ are $0.04$, $0.08$, $0.12$ and $0.24$, one to each pair, and they are the distinct values the local exponent of Figure~\ref{fig:simu_param}-(a) produces over the pairs at which the estimators were computed. The theory leads us to expect the least gain from two bandwidths at the first pair and the most at the last.

Figure~\ref{fig:autocov_ratio_lag1} shows the boxplots of the error ratio \eqref{eq:error_ratio_autocov} at these four pairs, for each of the twelve setups $(N, \lambda)$. As expected the ratio lies below one at every pair in almost every setup: over the $48$ pairs of location and setup, its median is below one in $40$ of them, exactly one in $6$ and above one in $2$. The gain is largest at $(0.8, 0.2)$, where the two exponents are furthest apart; the median there runs from $0.433$ to $0.911$ and is the lowest of the four in eleven of the twelve setups.

The ordering does not carry through the middle of the range. At $(0.4, 0.7)$, whose gap is the larger of the two intermediate ones, the median ratio exceeds the median at $(0.2, 0.4)$ in eleven of the twelve setups. The level of the two exponents seems to matter alongside their difference. The two pairs at which the gain is largest, $(0.8, 0.2)$ and $(0.2, 0.4)$, are the two that have an argument at $0.2$, the roughest of the four points at $H = 0.48$; the two at which it is smallest, $(0.4, 0.7)$ and $(0.7, 0.8)$, have neither. A bandwidth chosen for a smooth argument is most badly wrong at a rough one, so it is where the domain is roughest that a second bandwidth earns most.

The medians equal to one have the same origin. The ratio is exactly one whenever the adaptive rule selects the same bandwidth for the two arguments, which it does in none of the $4800$ replications at $(0.8, 0.2)$, against between $5$ and $41$ of the $400$ replications of a setup at $(0.7, 0.8)$ --- the most of the four pairs in eleven of the twelve setups. As a consequence the four pairs differ not only in the size of the gain but in how often there is any gain to be had.

Two features of the display are declared rather than left to be inferred. The estimator returns no value in $532$ of the $19200$ combinations of replication, location and setup, and those replications are absent from the boxes. The vertical axis stops at $1.5$, and $5456$ of the remaining $18668$ ratios lie at or above it, so the boxes describe the lower part of the distribution and not the whole of it.

\subsection{Adaptive curve prediction}\label{sec:num:prediction}

\color{magenta}
The predictor under study is the Tikhonov-regularised predictor $\mathfrak{X}^{(M_{n_0})}_{n_0+1}(t;\alpha,\sigma^2\Dd_{n_0})$ of \eqref{our_reg_blup}, with the unknown $\mu$, $c_0$, $c_1$ and $\sigma^2$ replaced by the adaptive estimators of Section~\ref{sec:emp_study:autocov}. We write $\widehat{\Xf}^{(M_{n_0})}_{n_0+1}(t;\alpha,\widehat\sigma^2\Dd_{n_0})$ for the resulting plug-in predictor. Throughout this section $\alpha$ denotes the Tikhonov regularisation parameter of \eqref{formula_tik_th} and no other quantity. The design weights $\varrho_{n_0,i}$ collected in $\Dd_{n_0}$ are those of \eqref{def_Qn}, specialised in Proposition~\ref{prop:tikhonov} to $M_{n_0}^{-1}$ under the equidistant common design and to $\{M_{n_0}\widehat g_{n_0,i}(T_{n_0,i})\}^{-1}$ under the independent design, with $\widehat g_{n_0,i}$ the leave-one-out Parzen--Rosenblatt estimator of \eqref{PR_weights}.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Simulation protocol\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
Each replication draws a complete functional time series. We generate $R = 400$ independent series for each of the twelve setups $(N, \lambda) \in \{150, 300, 600\} \times \{25, 50, 100, 200\}$, and within a series we predict the last curve from the one preceding it. The estimates entering the predictor are computed on the curves before the target, so that no information about the curve being predicted enters its own prediction.

A series is generated from the autoregressive model \eqref{blup:FTS_mod1}. The innovations are multifractional Brownian motions with the local Hölder exponent $H_t = 0.4 + 0.4\,t$, which rises across the domain from $0.4$ to $0.8$, and with Hölder constant $0.1$. Each innovation carries a centred Gaussian intercept of its own, of standard deviation $0.0224$, drawn once per curve. Without it every curve would leave the origin at the same point, the multifractional Brownian motion being pinned at zero there. The operator norm of the autoregressive operator is set to $0.5$, and a burn-in of $100$ curves is discarded.

Each series carries both designs on the same curves. Under the independent design the number of points per curve is drawn uniformly on the integers between $0.8\lambda$ and $1.2\lambda$, and the design points are independent draws from the uniform distribution on $(0,1]$; under the common design every curve is observed on the shared equidistant grid of $\lambda$ points. Both are observed with the same centred Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.007$, and the noiseless curve is retained alongside. As a consequence, running the study on each design in turn isolates the effect of the design, everything else being held fixed.

Every parameter is selected inside the replication, on that replication's own fit sample, so that a replication carries no information from any other. The bandwidths are selected by the adaptive rules of Section~\ref{sec:emp_study:autocov}, and the design-density bandwidth and $\alpha$ by cross-validation. One seed is drawn per replication rather than per curve, since the innovations must remain successive draws from a single advancing stream; the seed depends on the replication's identity and not on its position in the loop. Details of the two cross-validation procedures, and of the grids they search, are provided in the Supporting Information.
\color{black}

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Autoregressive effect\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
The adaptive predictor depends on adaptive estimates of the mean, covariance and lag-$1$ autocovariance functions. To describe the strength of dependence between the target curve and the curve preceding it, we compute a functional analogue of the autocorrelation function for real-valued time series. The Functional Autocorrelation Function (FACF) at lag~$\ell$, considered by \citet{horovath_2016}, is defined as
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blup:facf}
	\color{magenta}\widehat{\rho}_\ell =
	\|\widehat{c}_{N,\ell}\|\left[\int \widehat{c}_{N,0}(t,t)dt\right]^{-1},\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
where $\widehat{c}_{N,\ell}$ estimates $c_\ell$, and $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the standard norm in $\LL^2_\Hh$. Moreover, $0 \leq \widehat{\rho}_\ell \leq 1$. The FACF also serves as an empirical check for the presence of an autoregressive effect in the FTS. In fact, if there were no autoregressive effect, then $c_1$ would vanish, the operator $\Pi_{n_0,1}^\star$ of \eqref{our_reg_blup} would be null, and the predictor would reduce to the mean function $\mu$, the lagged curve contributing nothing.

Across the setups on which it was computed, the median of the lag-$1$ FACF runs from $0.251$ to $0.328$, so the autoregressive term is bounded away from zero everywhere in the study and the lagged curve does contribute. The histograms are presented in Supporting Information.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Weighted integrated squared error\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
The curve to be predicted is observed at its own design points $T_{n_0+1,i}$, $1\leq i \leq M_{n_0+1}$, and the predictor is scored there. Let $\varrho_{n_0+1,i}$ be the design weights of \eqref{def_Qn} formed on that curve, that is $M_{n_0+1}^{-1}$ under the equidistant common design and $\{M_{n_0+1}\widehat g_{n_0+1,i}(T_{n_0+1,i})\}^{-1}$ under the independent design. The weighted integrated squared error of the adaptive predictor is
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted_ise}
	\color{magenta}\mathrm{ISE}\big(\widehat{\Xf}^{(M_{n_0})}_{n_0+1}\big) = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{n_0+1}} \varrho_{n_0+1,i}\left\{Y_{n_0+1,i} - \widehat{\Xf}^{(M_{n_0})}_{n_0+1}\big(T_{n_0+1,i};\alpha,\widehat\sigma^2 \Dd_{n_0}\big)\right\}^2,\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
a discrete Riemann sum against the measure $Q_{n_0+1}$ of \eqref{def_Qn}, which weights each design point by the inverse of the local design density and so does not over-count regions where observations happen to concentrate. Replacing $Y_{n_0+1,i}$ by the noiseless $X_{n_0+1}(T_{n_0+1,i})$ gives a second variant; the two differ by the measurement-error variance, an additive offset that does not cancel in a ratio.

The same functional is the criterion by which $\alpha$ is selected: the cross-validation score of the Tikhonov selection is \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} evaluated on held-out curves. Definition, scoring and selection are therefore one object, not three.

Two details of the implementation are stated here, neither of them visible in \eqref{eq:weighted_ise}. The weights are normalised to sum to one exactly, which \eqref{def_Qn} requires and which the independent-design form of Proposition~\ref{prop:tikhonov} attains only in the limit. The estimated design density is floored at $10^{-6}$ before it is inverted, which bounds the weight a point in a sparse region can receive and stops a near-zero estimate carrying the score to infinity. Both guard the criterion against numerical degeneration, and neither alters the quantity \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} defines.

The quantity in \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} is one number per replication, so it is reported as a distribution over the $R = 400$ replications rather than as a pointwise curve. For each setup $(N,\lambda)$ and each design we report its median and interquartile range, in both variants. No replication is discarded: the score is finite on $400$ of $400$ replications in every setup and under both designs. The two designs are not read against each other, their weights being $M_{n_0+1}^{-1}$ in one case and $\{M_{n_0+1}\widehat g_{n_0+1,i}(T_{n_0+1,i})\}^{-1}$ in the other, so that the measure the score integrates against is not the same; the cross-design view is taken through ratios, which are normalised within a design. A decomposition into bias and variance is available under the common design alone, where the locations at which it is read are grid points at every $\lambda$ of the study; under the independent design each replication draws its own design points, and there is no shared location at which the predictions could be averaged.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Prediction accuracy\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
Table~\ref{tab:blup_benchmark_random} shows the median weighted integrated squared error of the adaptive predictor under the independent design, together with its interquartile range, over the twelve setups. The median runs from $0.00306$ to $0.00385$ and falls with $N$ at $\lambda = 25$ and at $\lambda = 50$, as expected of a predictor whose operators are estimated across curves. It does not fall at $\lambda = 200$, where it rises with $N$ instead and exceeds its $\lambda = 100$ value at every $N$, which may be surprising given that more points per curve are observed there. The plug-in operators are formed across curves rather than within one, so a finer grid on each curve does not by itself sharpen them, and at $(N, \lambda) = (600, 200)$, alone among the twelve setups, the selection of $\alpha$ is made on a trailing subset of the fit sample rather than on all of it.

Table~\ref{tab:blup_benchmark_common} presents the same quantities under the common design, where the median runs from $0.00276$ to $0.00396$. The behaviour at $\lambda = 200$ is the one just described: the median there exceeds its $\lambda = 100$ value at $N = 300$ and at $N = 600$, and equals it at $N = 150$. The two tables are not on a common scale, the design weights entering \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} differing between the two designs. Each design is therefore read against its own baseline below, and no comparison is drawn between the levels of the two tables.

Panel C of both tables holds the estimated mean curve, scored on the same measure and on the same replications. That curve is the limit of the predictor as $\alpha$ grows, the regularised operator vanishing and the lagged curve contributing nothing, so the ratio of the two scores measures what the lagged curve contributes. The median of the per-replication ratios runs from $1.06$ to $1.21$ under the independent design and from $1.14$ to $1.26$ under the common design, and it is the larger of the two in every one of the twelve setups. Ratios being normalised within a design, this is the one comparison the two tables do support.

Figure~\ref{fig:log_wise_ratio} shows the distribution behind those medians, and the gain is a shift of a widely overlapping distribution rather than a uniform improvement. The adaptive predictor is the closer of the two on $56.0$ to $62.7$ per cent of replications under the independent design and on $58.0$ to $64.8$ per cent under the common design, so on about two replications in five the estimated mean curve is the closer. The value at which the two are equally far from the target lies inside the interquartile box of all $24$ combinations of setup and design, while the median of the ratio lies above it in each. This is likely due to the strength of the dependence the study simulates: a single lagged curve carries limited information about the next one at a lag-$1$ FACF whose median is between $0.251$ and $0.328$.

The prediction error is read pointwise under the common design, at $t \in \{0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80\}$. The bias lies between $-0.0060$ and $0.0094$ at those four locations, against a standard deviation that rises across the domain in every setup, from between $0.0502$ and $0.0558$ at $t = 0.20$ to between $0.0858$ and $0.0980$ at $t = 0.80$. As expected, the error tracks the marginal variance of the innovation process, which grows across the domain as the local exponent rises from $0.4$ to $0.8$. The predictor's error standard deviation is below that of the estimated mean curve at all $48$ combinations of location and setup, and by the widest margin at $t = 0.40$ in every setup, where the ratio of the two lies between $0.703$ and $0.800$ against between $0.851$ and $0.957$ elsewhere. The gain is therefore concentrated in the middle of the domain, and it narrows at both ends.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Selected Tikhonov parameter\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
The parameter $\alpha$ is selected in each replication by cross-validation over a grid of $40$ points, log-spaced between $1.1\cdot 10^{-7}$ and $20.1$. Its median over the $400$ replications of a setup runs from $2.7\cdot 10^{-4}$ down to $3.9\cdot 10^{-5}$ under the independent design and from $3.9\cdot 10^{-5}$ down to $1.5\cdot 10^{-5}$ under the common design, and it never increases with either $N$ or $\lambda$. As expected, the criterion asks for less regularisation as the sample grows, the operators entering \eqref{our_reg_blup} being better conditioned there.

The selection is smaller under the common design in every one of the twelve setups, and it reaches the floor of the grid under that design alone. It does so in $22.8$ per cent of the replications at $(600, 25)$, in $16.8$ per cent at $(300, 25)$ and in $8.5$ per cent at $(600, 50)$, the share growing with $N$ and falling with $\lambda$; no independent-design setup reaches the floor at all. Observing every curve at the same $\lambda$ points is what conditions the operators well enough for the criterion to ask for less ridge than the grid offers. A selection on the floor is the grid choosing the parameter rather than the data, which is the restriction under which the common-design setups at the smaller $\lambda$ are to be read; at that floor the ridge is $1.1\cdot 10^{-7}$ and numerically none at all.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}Comparison with competing procedures\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
Under the independent design we compare our predictor with the procedure of \citet{rubin2020sparsely}, referred to as \texttt{RP20}. That procedure is also based on the best linear unbiased predictor: the mean is estimated by a local linear smoother, the lag-$\ell$ autocovariance by a local linear surface smoother away from the diagonal and by a local quadratic smoother on it, and the smoothing bandwidths are selected by $K$-fold cross-validation coupled with Bayesian optimisation. A single bandwidth is selected for the whole domain, whereas ours varies with the location. The comparison is confined to the independent design because \texttt{RP20} is constructed for sparsely and irregularly sampled curves, which is the regime the common grid removes.

The competitor is fitted on exactly the data our predictor is fitted on. For each replication we export the noisy observations of the curves preceding the target, together with the target curve at its own design points, in the form the implementation consumes; one file is written per replication. Both predictors are then fitted on that estimation sample and evaluated for the same target curve.

The two are scored on the evaluation points they share. \texttt{RP20} returns its prediction on a fixed grid while ours is evaluated at the target curve's own design points, so each design point is carried to its nearest grid point and the squared error of each predictor is integrated by the trapezoidal rule against the noiseless target curve. This score is not \eqref{eq:weighted_ise}: it is unweighted, and it is taken against a common evaluation set rather than against the design measure of the target curve. We refer to it as the trapezoidal error, and we compare the two predictors through the logarithm of its ratio, a value below zero indicating the smaller error for the adaptive predictor. The design weights of \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} would in any case cancel from such a ratio.
[TBC: the evaluation grid \texttt{RP20} returns is described in the superseded text as a regular grid of $241$ points with a B-spline projection used to reduce the cost of the Bayesian optimisation. Neither number nor the projection is attested in any current file, so both are omitted here; restate them from the MATLAB implementation once it has been run]

Under the common design we compare our predictor with the procedure of \citet{zhao2026functional}, referred to as \texttt{Z26}. That procedure estimates the autoregressive operator by a single Tikhonov regularisation,
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zhao_estimator}
	\color{magenta}\widehat{\psi}(\cdot\,;\alpha_N) = \widehat{C}_1 \left(\widehat{C}_0 + \alpha_N \mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1},\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
with $\alpha_N \to 0$, and predicts the next curve by $\widehat\mu + \widehat\psi(X_{n_0} - \widehat\mu)$. It dispenses with the projection onto the leading eigenfunctions of $C_0$ that a truncation-based estimator requires, so a single regularisation parameter is selected rather than two. The parameter is selected by cross-validation, as ours is, which makes the two procedures comparable on the choice of regularisation and not only on the prediction.

The comparison is confined to the common design because \eqref{eq:zhao_estimator} is defined for curves observed on a shared grid: the empirical operators $\widehat{C}_0$ and $\widehat{C}_1$ are then formed as $\lambda \times \lambda$ matrices from the discretised curves, without any preliminary smoothing. Under the independent design no two curves share their design points, and the operators would have to be built from smoothed curves, which would compare our smoothing with itself rather than the two predictors.

Both procedures are fitted on the curves preceding the target and predict the same target curve. Since both are evaluated on the shared grid, no interpolation is needed and the two are scored by \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} itself, whose weights reduce to $\lambda^{-1}$ there. We compare them through the logarithm of the ratio of their scores.

\subsection{Real data applications}\label{sec:num:realdata}

\color{magenta}
Forecasting electricity consumption and production is essential to keeping the electricity system in balance, since it reduces the costs of storage and of overproduction, and it is a harder problem for hydropower and photovoltaics, whose output varies through the day with the weather. Forecasting intraday traded volume answers a different need: an order worked against the volume-weighted average price has to be sliced against the volume it expects to meet, and a liquidity provider sizes inventory against the same quantity. Functional time series provide an effective framework for both, the more so when the prediction adapts to the regularity of the underlying process, as our procedure does.

The two applications are chosen to exercise the two designs of \assrefH{H:model:Tni}. The volume curves are observed at the instants at which trades occur, which differ from one day to the next, so their design is random and irregular and the design density has to be estimated; the consumption curves are recorded on the same equidistant quarter-hourly grid every day, so their design is common and the weights of \eqref{def_Qn} reduce to $M_{n_0+1}^{-1}$. This is the split under which each competing procedure was confined to one design in Section~\ref{sec:num:prediction}, and the reader meets it here a second time, on real curves rather than simulated ones. In both applications the last curve of the series is held out entirely and predicted from the one preceding it, every estimate entering the predictor being computed on the curves before the target, exactly as in the simulation study. Photovoltaic and hydroelectric production curves are analysed under the same protocol and are presented in the Supporting Information.
\color{black}

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}NOTPERP intraday volume curves\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
We consider the NOTPERP perpetual futures contract, written on the Notcoin token and quoted in USDT on the ByBit exchange, whose complete trade record is published as one compressed file per UTC calendar day at \url{https://public.bybit.com/trading/NOTPERP/}. The archive holds $712$ consecutive days, from 18 September 2024 to 30 August 2026. Cryptocurrency markets trade continuously, so the UTC calendar day is the natural segmentation unit and each day $n$ yields one curve. Let $\tau_{n,i}$ denote the timestamp in seconds of the $i$-th trade of day $n$, and $a_n$ the timestamp of midnight UTC on that day. The observation times are
\color{black}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:notperp:tni}
	\color{magenta}T_{n,i} = \frac{\tau_{n,i} - a_n}{86400} \in (0,1],\color{black}
\end{equation}
\color{magenta}
where $86400$ is the number of seconds in a day. The response at $T_{n,i}$ is the logarithm of the notional traded since midnight up to and including that trade, so the level of a curve is the day's liquidity and its shape is when in the day that liquidity arrives. Predicting the next such curve is what an execution schedule requires, an order worked against the volume-weighted average price having to be sliced against the volume it expects to meet.

The daily level of the series is not stationary over the whole archive, the token having been listed in September 2024 and its liquidity having declined since. We therefore retain the longest run of days ending at the last one over which a KPSS test does not reject a level null, which is the $430$ days from 27 June 2025; a linear trend accounts for $0.027$ of the daily variation over that window, against $0.093$ over the archive. Six outlying curves are then removed by an iterative functional boxplot, and the number of observations per curve is capped at $200$ by a uniform draw among a curve's trade instants. The sample is $N = 424$ curves carrying $84\,131$ observations, with $M_n$ between $105$ and $200$ and $198.4$ points per curve on average. The cleaning is described in the Supporting Information.

The irregularity of this design is native. Trades arrive at instants that differ from one day to the next, and their number per day spans two orders of magnitude, from $132$ to $49\,768$ over the raw archive. No filtering rule is applied to obtain irregular observation times and none is needed, which is why this contract was chosen: the times are what the market delivers, and the second branch of \assrefH{H:model:Tni} is satisfied as it stands rather than by construction. The design density is accordingly estimated by the leave-one-out estimator of \eqref{PR_weights} rather than assumed flat, and the cap at $200$ truncates $M_n$ from above without regularising the times, the draw being uniform among a curve's own instants and the response cumulative. As a consequence the $M_n$ vary from curve to curve, as \assrefH{H:model:Mn} allows.

The lag-$1$ FACF \eqref{eq:blup:facf}, computed on the curves preceding the target, is $0.337$. It lies above the median of every setup of the simulation study, which runs from $0.251$ to $0.328$, so the autoregressive term contributes at least as much here as in the study that calibrated it. The estimated local Hölder exponent runs from $0.254$ to $1$ over the domain, with a median of $0.806$, and it reaches the upper end of the interval to which the estimator is clamped at $29$ of the $99$ points at which it is evaluated. The estimator recovers the level of the exponent rather than its variation, so what these estimates support is that the curves are smooth in the sense of the model, and not any particular profile across the day; a cumulative response is smoother than the trades it is built from, which is consistent with a level this high. The profile is presented in Supporting Information.

Figure~\ref{fig:notperp_volume_pred} shows the last curve of the sample against its adaptive prediction, obtained from the curve preceding it. The regularisation parameter is selected within a grid of $60$ points, log-spaced between $2.1\cdot 10^{-9}$ and $54.6$, and the selection is interior, at $\alpha = 0.016$. The cross-validation criterion reads $0.4626$ there against $0.5616$ at the upper end of the grid, where the regularised operator vanishes and the prediction reduces to the estimated mean function. The lagged curve is therefore worth $17.6$ per cent of the cross-validated error, and this is the application's central result: on a quantity whose dependence is carried by the daily level, the conditioning curve is not discarded by the criterion that is free to discard it.

On the held-out curve itself, the squared error of the prediction averaged over the target's $198$ observation points is $1.211$ times smaller than that of the estimated mean curve, and the prediction is the closer of the two at $98.0$ per cent of those points. That ratio sits at the top of the range from $1.06$ to $1.21$ which the simulation study reports under the independent design. The average is unweighted rather than taken from \eqref{eq:weighted_ise}: at the selected design-density bandwidth the leave-one-out estimate vanishes at $21$ of the $198$ points, so the floored weights of \eqref{eq:weighted_ise} would carry the whole of the score on those points alone.

The gain lies in the shape of the curve and not in its level. The day being predicted was an unusually quiet one, sitting between $1.38$ and $2.17$ in logarithm below the estimated mean function at every point of the domain, while the prediction departs from that mean function by at most $0.27$. The adaptive predictor therefore tracks the timing of the day's activity and understates how little of it there was, which is what a single lagged curve can carry at a lag-$1$ FACF of this size.

\subsubsection{\color{magenta}French electricity consumption curves\color{black}}

\color{magenta}
We analyse daily curves of national electricity consumption in France, obtained from the ODR\'E platform \citep{odre_data} over the period from 1 June to 30 September 2024. The dataset holds $122$ curves, one for each day of the period, each recorded at $96$ equally spaced quarter-hourly points, and every value is divided by the maximum consumption observed over the period, so that the curves take values in $[0.546, 1]$.

Every day is recorded on the same grid, so $M_n = \lambda = 96$ for all $n$ and the first branch of \assrefH{H:model:Tni} holds as stated; the design weights of \eqref{def_Qn} reduce to $96^{-1}$ and no design density is estimated.
